# Beyond Banks: What Would It Take to Finance Uganda’s US$500 Billion Economy?

*By Kenneth Legesi, CFA | CEO & CIO, Oakstone Capital (OAC)*

A Ugandan agro-processor has export buyers, farmers ready to supply it and a plan for a new factory. A bank may finance its working capital. The business still needs construction equity, machinery finance, reliable power and an investor willing to wait for the factory to produce at scale.

Multiply that problem across industrial parks, energy, housing, tourism, minerals and technology. This is the capital formation challenge behind Uganda’s target of a **US$500 billion economy by 2040**—about **UGX 1,850 trillion** at the constant illustrative exchange rate used below.[1]

**Where will the capital come from? Not from banks alone.** Before allocating it among financiers, however, we need to be more precise about how much investment the target implies.

## The size of the task: investment is a flow, GDP is an outcome

GDP is the value an economy produces **each year**. Gross capital formation is the **annual investment flow** into fixed assets such as buildings, equipment and infrastructure, plus changes in inventories. It does not measure a stock of money sitting in an account. New investment can add productive capacity, but some replaces worn-out assets, some takes years to operate, and some produces disappointing returns. More spending does not automatically create proportionate GDP.

World Bank national accounts put Uganda’s **2025 gross capital formation at 22.65% of GDP**, approximately **US$14.0 billion (UGX 51.6 trillion)** against GDP of **US$62.0 billion (UGX 227.9 trillion)**.[2] Fixed capital formation, excluding inventory changes, was **21.86% of GDP**. The financing model in this article uses gross capital formation throughout, so its historical and forward estimates are comparable.

Over **2015–2025**, Uganda’s gross capital formation averaged **24.0% of GDP** while real GDP growth averaged **5.0% a year**—my calculations from the World Bank’s annual series.[2] That is the **business-as-usual investment-rate baseline** for this exercise. Maintaining 24% as GDP grows would still require far more shillings and dollars each year than Uganda invests today. It is therefore a *conditional* baseline, not a guarantee that current financing channels will automatically scale.

Going from **US$62.0 billion (UGX 227.9 trillion)** in 2025 to **US$500 billion (illustratively UGX 1,850 trillion)** in 2040 requires **14.9% compound annual growth in nominal US-dollar GDP**. That is not the same as real growth. Even sustained **8% real annual growth** would require roughly **6.4% a year in the dollar price of Ugandan output**, reflecting domestic GDP inflation and exchange-rate movements, to reach the dollar target. Investment, exports, productivity and macroeconomic stability must therefore be modelled together.

A rough capital-efficiency check is instructive. The historical averages give a ratio of **24% investment to 5% real growth, or about 4.8 units of investment share per unit of growth**. This is *not* a measured causal capital-output coefficient: inventories, depreciation, lags, shocks and productivity all complicate it. As a simple sensitivity, achieving **8% real growth** at an efficiency ratio of **4.8** would point to investment of roughly **38% of GDP**. At an improved ratio of **4.0**, it points to **32%**; at **3.5**, **28%**. Improving the quality of investment matters almost as much as raising its quantity.

## An initial national investment model

My proposed **base case** begins with Uganda’s historical **24% of GDP** investment rate and tests a gradual rise to **30% by 2030, 35% by 2035 and 40% by 2040**. The GDP checkpoints below are Oakstone Capital (OAC) scenarios for testing; only the **US$500 billion in 2040** is the government target.[1] Investment ratios are financing requirements to examine, **not forecasts that those ratios will cause the GDP path**.

| Cycle endpoint | GDP checkpoint | If historical 24% persists: annual investment | OAC base-case investment rate and annual flow | Additional annual flow above 24% |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| **2025 observed** | **US$62.0bn / UGX 227.9tn** | Historical comparison only | **22.65%: US$14.0bn / UGX 51.6tn** | — |
| **2026–2030: build** | **US$120bn / ~UGX 444tn** | **US$28.8bn / ~UGX 106.6tn** | **30%: US$36.0bn / ~UGX 133.2tn** | **US$7.2bn / ~UGX 26.6tn** |
| **2031–2035: scale** | **US$250bn / ~UGX 925tn** | **US$60.0bn / ~UGX 222tn** | **35%: US$87.5bn / ~UGX 323.8tn** | **US$27.5bn / ~UGX 101.8tn** |
| **2036–2040: expand** | **US$500bn / ~UGX 1,850tn** | **US$120bn / ~UGX 444tn** | **40%: US$200bn / ~UGX 740tn** | **US$80bn / ~UGX 296tn** |

A higher-investment case adds **five percentage points** to the base-case rate at each endpoint: **35% by 2030** would require **US$42bn (UGX 155.4tn)** a year; **40% by 2035**, **US$100bn (UGX 370tn)**; and **45% by 2040**, **US$225bn (UGX 832.5tn)**. It buys no assured GDP result if project quality, exports or productivity fail to improve.

Under straight-line annual interpolation of the GDP checkpoints and investment ratios from 2026 through 2040, cumulative gross capital formation is approximately **US$1.23 trillion (UGX 4,540 trillion)**, of which about **US$393 billion (UGX 1,455 trillion)** is above the conditional 24%-of-GDP path. These are **sums of annual flows over 15 years**, not a cheque to raise now, and not necessarily a foreign-financing gap. Firms reinvest earnings, loans are repaid and recycled, and domestic savings can rise. Conversely, the GDP path itself is exceptionally demanding; if it is missed, the investment totals change.

The national model must therefore have four connected ledgers: **real growth and productivity; investment needs and capital efficiency by sector; domestic savings plus net external finance; and an actual transaction pipeline**. Each year should reconcile the national accounts to projects that reach financial close and ultimately operate. Exchange-rate, execution and capital-efficiency sensitivities are essential.

For all future amounts, **UGX equivalents use a fixed presentation convention of UGX 3,700 per US$1**, not a forecast exchange rate. The 2025 UGX and USD national-accounts values are separately reported actuals, so they need not translate at precisely that convention.

## What part is already funded, and what must be additional?

The **US$14.0 billion (UGX 51.6 trillion)** invested in 2025 is the observed starting flow. On the hypothetical **US$120 billion (UGX 444 trillion)** economy in 2030, simply keeping the historical 24% rate would mean **US$28.8 billion (UGX 106.6 trillion)** in annual investment. That **US$14.8 billion (roughly UGX 55 trillion)** rise from the 2025 actual is *business-as-usual growth in the investment flow conditional on GDP nearly doubling*. It still needs financing and is not already secured.

The **additional policy challenge in the 2030 base case** is the next **US$7.2 billion (UGX 26.6 trillion)** a year that lifts investment from 24% to 30% of GDP. That is the amount to allocate to *new or expanded* financing channels. Treating all US$36 billion as a fresh fundraising target would ignore the investment that normally occurs; treating the US$28.8 billion baseline as guaranteed would be equally careless.

The stock of domestic financial assets shows opportunity and constraints. Retirement schemes held **UGX 35.6 trillion (US$9.6 billion)** as of March 2026; collective investment schemes held **UGX 7.06 trillion (US$1.9 billion)** in September 2026.[3][4] These belong to savers. UDB reported **UGX 2.26 trillion (US$0.61 billion)** in assets and **UGX 502.2 billion (US$0.14 billion)** disbursed in 2025.[5] Neither UDB’s balance sheet nor pension assets can be counted as fresh annual national investment. Payments turnover and guarantees cannot be added to investment flows either.

The first five domestic lines amount to **US$2.4 billion (UGX 8.9 trillion)**; diaspora and the other external lines make **US$4.8 billion (UGX 17.8 trillion)**. The allocation is deliberately a *starting hypothesis*. By 2028, it should be revised against actual subscriptions, signed FDI projects, funded DFI facilities, bank lending and private-market closes. Bank loan growth, FDI inflows and pension subscriptions are not identical to national-accounts capital formation; the project-level reconciliation must identify which flows fund *new domestic productive assets* rather than refinancing, financial-asset trades or imports that do not create local capacity.

## Where Uganda is strongest—and weakest

**The strongest link today is mobilising and intermediating savings.** The banking and payments networks exist; retirement assets have grown to **UGX 35.6 trillion (US$9.6 billion)**; collective investment schemes have reached **UGX 7.06 trillion (US$1.9 billion)** and more than **220,000 funded accounts**.[3][4] There is a functioning UDB and an Agricultural Credit Facility. Uganda need not begin by inventing savers.

**The weakest link is the conversion of savings into a sustained pipeline of investable private assets.** URBRA reports **82% of scheme assets** in government securities for FY2024/25; CMA reports **86.8% of collective investment scheme assets** in government bonds at December 2025.[10][11] Those choices can be rational for savers given yields, liquidity and mandates. They also signal a shortage of private instruments able to compete on a risk-adjusted basis. UDB already offers project preparation, but its **UGX 0.50 trillion (US$0.14 billion)** annual disbursement scale illustrates how far a national pipeline must grow.[5][12]

This judgment is an inference from allocation and issuance evidence, not a claim that every private project is viable or that government securities are unproductive. The **first intervention priority** should be project preparation and private issuance: feasibility, permits and land, reliable demand, financial models, standard contracts, credit enhancement, independent assessment and enforcement. Resolving verified public arrears and payment disputes is part of this work because uncertain receivables make both enterprises and projects less financeable.

The ten connected workstreams follow from that diagnosis: **capital formation governance; fiscal leverage and public-capital reform; arrears resolution; guarantees; creditworthiness information; capital-market development; pension and institutional-capital mobilisation; sector financing platforms; project preparation and infrastructure finance; and legal and market infrastructure reform.** A National Capital Formation Council could coordinate existing institutions around a public pipeline and scorecard, with named workstream owners. It should clear blockages, not create another approval gate.

## What other paths tell us

A comparator must show both **how much was invested** and **what made it productive**. On a common World Bank basis, the 2010–2024 average gross-capital-formation and real-growth rates were approximately: **Viet Nam 32.0% investment and 6.1% growth; Malaysia 23.7% and 4.6%; Kenya 20.4% and 4.9%; South Africa 16.5% and 1.3%; Uganda 25.4% and 5.1%**. These are simple averages of annual series, not causal estimates.[13]

Viet Nam combined sustained high investment with trade opening, export manufacturing, FDI and links to global value chains. Malaysia’s transformation combined export-oriented industrialisation, foreign investment, infrastructure and capable institutions. Kenya expanded financial access and payments, but its investment and growth record shows that inclusion alone does not establish an industrial investment pipeline. South Africa has deep capital markets and credit, yet infrastructure and logistics constraints have held back investment and output.[14][15][16]

Uganda’s own history makes the same point: its **25.4% average investment ratio in 2010–2024** was higher than Malaysia’s **23.7%**, yet this did not transform its industrial and export base at the required pace. **The transferable path is a combination of sustained investment, productive export opportunities, infrastructure reliability, firm capabilities and institutions that execute.** Capital depth without functioning assets is insufficient; high spending without productivity is insufficient.

## Test the programme—and Oakstone’s contribution

The scorecard starts with an observed **2025 investment rate of 22.65%, US$14.0bn (UGX 51.6tn)** and **bank private credit of 12.55% of GDP**.[2][17] Retirement and CIS assets, UDB disbursements, FDI and the concentration in government securities provide additional baselines. There is **no consolidated national, transaction-level baseline identified** for the number of projects reaching financial close, private capital mobilised *net of double counting*, average tenor and currency hedge cost, fiscal guarantee losses, and post-close exports, jobs and repayments. Building that register is itself an early deliverable.

At **Oakstone Capital (OAC)**, we can contribute to the workstreams where transaction capability is most scarce. Our project-finance modelling and preparation work supports **workstream 9**; renewable-energy and industrial-cluster structuring supports the **sector platforms in workstream 8**; private-credit and alternative-investment fund design connects **workstreams 6 and 7**; and our access-to-finance and diligence work helps **workstream 5**. Our proposed national investment model and scorecard would support **workstream 1**, while transaction-level risk allocation can inform the guarantee and public-capital work in **workstreams 2 and 4**.

Our next practical contribution is to publish the model assumptions and source register, prepare an initial portfolio with partners, test investable structures with regulated managers and lenders, and report which transactions close. This is a proposed programme, not a claim of a government mandate or secured financing.

The route to US$500 billion is demanding, and the nominal-dollar target is especially sensitive to productivity and the exchange rate. Uganda can still make the financing challenge actionable. **Measure the investment already occurring. Build from the historical rate. Prepare the assets that can use additional capital. Match each risk to its proper holder. Then measure what the completed investment produces.**
